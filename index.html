StockStalker

A command-line research pipeline. One command builds a tear sheet of price signals, risk, factor exposure, a reverse DCF from public financials, options for F&O stocks and headline sentiment for any NSE stock; full fundamentals are hand-built for one name. Verified end to end on an F&O large-cap, a non-F&O mid-cap and Gulf Oil Lubricants, the hand-researched subject. The three-statement model is built by hand for researched companies only.

Three parts: Screen: 3 names, 1 of 3 held up. Tear sheet: one command, price signals for any NSE stock, full fundamentals for one. Lab: separate repo, testing phase. Its evidence so far: some one-day rules held up on training data, none convincingly on the one-week horizon.

  1. ScreenTrend, momentum, volume, liquidity
  2. ValuationReverse DCF: what the price assumes
  3. ModelLinked three-statement forecast
  4. RiskMonte Carlo VaR, Kupiec backtest
  5. FactorsIndian Fama-French + momentum
  6. OptionsImplied vol from real NSE chains

Risk

10 vs 5 VaR breaches vs expected at 99%

On RELIANCE, the constant-volatility normal VaR fails its own backtest: Kupiec rejects it (p = 0.048). The tails are fatter than the model assumes. Tested out of sample on GULFOILLUB (pre-registered, 2,506 test days, refit every 20 days), the constant-volatility normal model still fails at 99% (42 breaches vs about 25 expected, p = 0.002); a GARCH-t model passes (21 vs about 25, p = 0.40).

196 / 704 NIFTY quotes break no-arbitrage before filtering, one 16 Oct 2026 snapshot

On one real captured NSE option chain (16 Oct 2026; for RELIANCE 25 of 218 break the floor and 18 more have no two-sided quote), raw quotes violated no-arbitrage bounds routinely within it, so filtering is not optional. Technical screen

1 of 3 names beat buy-and-hold after costs

Including Indian delivery costs (STT, stamp duty, slippage), the trend/momentum/volume screen mostly loses to simply holding. Its one surviving win also beats being long the same number of days at random: at the 90th percentile of 2,000 random draws, better than most random picks of the same exposure. With three names, one result that high happens by chance about a quarter of the time. A second apparent win, before costs, was itself that kind of random-exposure fluke (bottom 5th percentile) and didn’t survive costs either.

Portfolio optimisation

+420% vs +256% equal weight vs max-Sharpe, both after costs, 126 folds

On 29 large caps, equal weight clearly beats mean-variance optimisation out of sample: +427% vs +309% before costs, +420% vs +256% after costs, with both portfolios paying to rebalance. Costs widen the gap, since the optimizer’s own turnover (54% per fold vs 5%) costs more than the raw cost-rate difference suggests. Robust across risk-free rates and training windows.

Research

Submitted to a conference, 2026

How Test-Set Peeking Inflates Benchmark Comparisons

Choosing the “best single model” after looking at its test score gives it a partly-luck advantage. Using CRAFT, a machine-learning pipeline, the paper measures that advantage against a cross-validation baseline that never sees the test score. Across 163 public datasets in three benchmark collections, changing the comparison model changes which method looks ahead in 31% of cases.

What I got wrong

Mistakes found by testing my own work, and what changed.

  1. Reverse DCF. Held one year’s reinvestment rate flat for ten years. Growth and reinvestment move together through return on capital, so v2 derives one from the other. See the fix →
  2. Screen. Expected it to beat buy-and-hold. After real Indian trading costs, only 1 of 3 names does. See the test →
  3. Risk. Expected a 99% VaR model to hold at its own confidence level. On RELIANCE over 500 days, the constant-volatility normal 99% risk line was breached 10 times against about 5 expected; the Kupiec backtest rejects it (p = 0.048). A pre-registered out-of-sample test on GULFOILLUB (2,506 days, refit every 20) gives the same answer for that model (42 vs about 25, p = 0.002), while a GARCH-t model passes (21 vs about 25, p = 0.40).
